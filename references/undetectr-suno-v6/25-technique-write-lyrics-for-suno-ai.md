Most people write lyrics for Suno AI the same way they would write lyrics for a human singer. That is the first mistake. A human vocalist interprets words — they add emotion, adjust phrasing, improvise around awkward syllable counts. **Suno interprets literally.** Every word you type gets processed at face value, with no artistic license to smooth over clunky phrasing or forced rhymes.

This means the difference between a Suno track that sounds polished and one that sounds like garbled nonsense comes down to how you write the lyrics. Not the style prompt. Not the genre tag. The actual words you feed into the lyrics field determine whether the vocal delivery sounds natural or like an AI struggling through a tongue twister.

## Can Suno Write Lyrics for You?

Yes. Suno can write the whole lyric sheet, and as of October 2026 it does it in three places. Suno’s own capability page says Simple Mode “writes the lyrics, picks a style, and generates a finished song,” and that writing lyrics is part of the free tier. What changes between the three is how much say you get over the words before you spend credits on them.

![Can Suno write lyrics for you: Simple Mode writes everything, Write with Suno drafts lyrics in Custom Mode with the ReMi or classic style, the July 2026 lyrics editor adds a Lyricist and plain-language edits, or paste your own words](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/can-suno-write-lyrics.webp)

- **Simple Mode.** Describe the song in a sentence and Suno writes the lyrics, the style and the structure, then makes two takes. You cannot choose a single line: the box is for a description of the song, and Suno writes its own words. For your exact words, use Custom Mode.
- **Write with Suno.** In Custom Mode, Suno generates lyrics from a topic or description. Suno lists two creative styles: the ReMi lyric model “for edgier, less expected results,” or a classic style. You can then co-write line by line, keeping what works and reworking the rest, before anything is sung.
- **The lyrics editor (July 2026).** Suno’s redesigned web lyrics editor added a **Lyricist**: you add examples of your own lyrics and it helps write new ones “with the same vibe.” It also takes plain-language edits (“make this line funnier”), suggests rhymes when you highlight a word, and offers song structure labels such as Verse and Outro.

### How to get Suno to write lyrics for you

1. Go to the Create page and choose **Custom**. On the web, several V6 walkthroughs show the toggle reading Simple, Advanced and Sounds; Advanced is the old Custom Mode, while the iOS app and Suno’s help articles still say Custom.
2. In the lyrics area, use **Write with Suno** and describe what the song is about in one or two sentences: who is singing, to whom, and what happened.
3. Pick the style. ReMi gives stranger lines worth mining; the classic style gives safer, more predictable ones.
4. Edit before you generate. Cut any line you would not say out loud, number the verses, and check the syllable counts with the rules below. Changing the text costs nothing; every generation costs 10 credits.
5. Add the Style, then press Create and listen to both takes before you change anything.

## Why Lyrics Matter More in AI Music

When a human singer receives lyrics, they apply decades of musical training and intuition. They know when to stretch a word, when to clip a syllable, when to breathe, when to push through a phrase. They compensate for poorly written lyrics with vocal technique. An AI music generator has none of this.

Suno's vocal model processes lyrics as a sequence of phonemes mapped to a melodic line. It generates the vocal delivery based on statistical patterns from its training data. If you give it a word with seven syllables followed by a word with two syllables, it will attempt to fit both into the melodic phrase — often producing an unnatural rush on the long word or an awkward stretch on the short one. There is no "feel" to compensate.

This is why two people can use the exact same [style prompt](./29-technique-general-prompts-guide.md), the exact same genre, and the exact same Suno version — and get wildly different results based solely on lyrics. The style prompt sets the musical context. The lyrics determine whether the vocals sound like a real singer or a text-to-speech engine fighting through unfamiliar words.

The good news? Once you understand how Suno processes lyrics, you can write specifically for it. And the output quality jumps dramatically. The techniques in this guide are the difference between "that sounds kind of AI-ish" and "wait, that was made with Suno?"

## The Conversational Rule: Write Like You Speak

The single most impactful lyric-writing rule for Suno: **write like you are talking to someone, not like you are writing poetry.** Conversational language uses naturally flowing syllable patterns, common vocabulary, and predictable stress patterns. Suno handles all of these well because its training data is dominated by popular music, which trends heavily toward conversational phrasing.

Compare these two approaches to the same idea. Poetic style: "Beneath the obsidian canopy of midnight's ephemeral embrace, I wander." Conversational style: "Under the dark sky tonight, I'm walking alone." The conversational version has clear syllable patterns, common words Suno knows how to pronounce flawlessly, and a natural rhythm that maps easily to a melodic line.

This does not mean your lyrics need to be simplistic or lack depth. Some of the most powerful songs ever written use plain language. "Let it be" — three common words. "I can't get no satisfaction" — everyday vocabulary. "Somebody that I used to know" — a sentence you might actually say. Emotional impact comes from meaning and melody, not from vocabulary complexity.

The conversational rule also prevents the most common Suno lyric failure: **mumbling**. When Suno encounters complex or unusual vocabulary, it often produces garbled vocal output that sounds like the singer is mumbling through the words. This happens because the model cannot find a confident phoneme mapping for the word and hedges between multiple pronunciations. Stick to words you would use in a text message and mumbling virtually disappears.

## Structural Tags: Give Suno a Blueprint

![Suno structure tags blueprint showing Intro, Verse, Pre-Chorus, Chorus, Bridge, and Outro with line counts and energy curve](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/structure-tags-blueprint.webp)

Structure tags are the single most underused feature in [Suno Custom Mode](./26-technique-custom-mode-guide.md). They tell the AI exactly how to organize the song — where to build energy, where to pull back, where to repeat the hook. Without them, Suno guesses at structure, and the guesses are often wrong.

The essential tags that Suno recognizes and responds to consistently:

- **[Intro]** — instrumental opening, sets the mood. Keep lyrics minimal or omit them entirely for instrumental intros
- **[Verse 1], [Verse 2], [Verse 3]** — main storytelling sections. Keep verses at 4-8 lines with consistent syllable counts across all verses
- **[Pre-Chorus]** — the build before the chorus. 2-4 lines that create tension and lead naturally into the hook. Energy should escalate
- **[Chorus]** — the emotional and melodic peak. 4-6 lines maximum. This is where your hook lives. Repeat the chorus tag multiple times in your lyrics
- **[Bridge]** — contrast section. Different melody, different perspective. 4 lines, typically placed after the second chorus
- **[Outro]** — wind-down. Can repeat chorus elements at lower energy or introduce a final closing thought. 2-4 lines
- **[Instrumental]** — tells Suno to generate music without vocals. Useful for intros, transitions, or solos
- **[Break]** — a momentary pause or stripped-back section. Good for creating dynamic contrast

A well-tagged lyric sheet gives Suno an architectural blueprint for the entire song. The AI knows where each section begins and ends, what energy level to target, and how to transition between sections. Without these tags, you are relying on Suno to infer structure from your text alone — and it frequently misjudges where the chorus should begin or how long the verse should run.

One critical detail: **always number your verses.** Using [Verse] without a number for every verse can cause Suno to generate the same melody for each verse section. [Verse 1], [Verse 2], and [Verse 3] signal that these are related but distinct sections, prompting slight melodic variations that sound more natural and less repetitive.

**On V6, treat tags as strong hints, not commands.** Suno has never published an official tag list: its V6 FAQ and lyric help articles name no bracket tags at all. The closest thing to official support is the July 2026 lyrics editor, which offers song structure labels such as Verse and Outro “to organize your lyrics [and] tell Suno how you want the song to flow.” Tags land most reliably when the Style field describes the same arrangement, and a short description inside the bracket, such as `[Verse 1: soft, close vocal]`, gives V6 more to work with than a bare label. If your intro hums for a minute or the song cuts off mid-line, our [V6 intro and ending guide](./06-v6-intro-outro-prompts.md) covers those two edges.

## Brackets vs Parentheses: Ad-libs, Backing Vocals and Numbers on V6

The lyrics box holds two kinds of text: words Suno should sing and instructions it should only read. Mixing them up is the most common reason Suno sings something you never meant as a lyric. Suno does not document any of this, so what follows are the conventions its users rely on.

![Suno V6 lyrics box cheat sheet: square brackets are read not sung, parentheses are sung as backing vocals, one line is one phrase, write times like 3:15 as three fifteen and spell acronyms like D-M-V](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/suno-v6-lyrics-box-cheat-sheet.webp)

- **[Square brackets] are read, not sung.** Section labels, the singer for a line in a duet, and two- or three-word performance notes go here. Keep them short: users report that a bracket which reads like a sentence is more likely to be sung.
- **(Parentheses) are sung,** usually as a backing vocal or echo underneath the lead line. This is where ad-libs and call-and-response belong: `Come home (come home)`. A r/SunoAI user noted in July 2025 that instructions put in parentheses sometimes get sung as words, and the forum’s June 2026 thread on the question gives the same rule: parentheses for backing vocals and echoes only.
- **Ad-libs only when you write them.** The most consistent good news on the V6 launch threads was that V6 stopped stacking ad-libs and repeating verses you did not write. If you want a “yeah” after the hook, write it in parentheses where you want it.
- **Numbers, times and acronyms the way they are sung.** A digit leaves the reading to the model: “3:15” could come out as “three fifteen” or something stranger, and “2019” has two common readings. Write “three fifteen” and “twenty nineteen”. Spell acronyms you want sung letter by letter with hyphens, “D-M-V”, and write the ones said as words, like “NASA”, as words.
- **Who sings which line.** In a duet, put the singer in the bracket above the lines, not in the lines. Our [V6 duet guide](./03-v6-duet-prompts.md) has a full singer-and-line sheet.

## Syllable Matching for Natural Rhythm

![Syllable matching comparison showing consistent vs inconsistent line lengths plus syllable-per-line targets by genre](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/syllable-matching.webp)

Syllable consistency is the technique that separates Suno lyrics that flow naturally from lyrics that sound forced and awkward. The principle is simple: **within each section, keep your line lengths roughly consistent.** If line one of your verse has 8 syllables, lines two through four should also hover around 8 syllables (plus or minus 1-2).

Here is why this matters technically. When Suno generates the melody for a section, it creates a rhythmic pattern based on the first few lines it encounters. If line one has 8 syllables and line two suddenly has 14, the AI must either compress 14 syllables into the same melodic space (producing rushed, unclear vocals) or extend the melodic phrase (breaking the rhythmic pattern it already established). Neither option sounds good.

The chorus is where syllable matching matters most. Your hook line — the one phrase you want listeners to remember — should be 6-10 words, and every time the chorus repeats, those lines should have identical syllable counts. Not roughly similar. Identical. The chorus melody locks in on the first occurrence, and any syllable mismatch in subsequent choruses will sound like the singer is stumbling.

A practical technique: write your verse, then count the syllables in each line. If you see 8, 7, 12, 8 — that third line is a problem. Either split it into two lines or rewrite it to hit 8-9 syllables. This ten-second check dramatically improves output quality. Tap out the rhythm on your desk as you read the lyrics aloud. If your finger taps at a consistent tempo, Suno will deliver the lines at a consistent tempo.

## Words and Patterns to Avoid

![Comparison of words to avoid in Suno lyrics versus power words Suno handles well for clean vocal output](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/words-to-avoid-vs-power-words.webp)

Certain words and patterns consistently cause problems in Suno's vocal output. Knowing what to avoid saves you from wasting generations on lyrics that will never sound clean no matter how many times you regenerate.

**Tongue twisters and consonant clusters.** Phrases like "sixth street's strength" or "crisps crumbled quickly" are difficult for even human singers. For Suno, they are nearly impossible. Rapid consonant clusters (str, scr, spl, thr) in succession cause the vocal model to produce blurred, muddy output. When you need to use these sounds, space them out with vowel-heavy words between them.

**Unusual proper nouns.** Names like "Krzyzewski" or "Schwarzenegger" or place names from non-English languages will almost certainly be mispronounced. Suno's phoneme mapping works best with English-standard pronunciation patterns. If you need to reference something with an unusual name, either spell it phonetically or use a simpler substitute.

**Overly complex rhyme schemes.** Internal rhymes, triple rhymes, and slant rhymes that require precise emphasis to work can confuse the model. "I found the sound underground" — three rhyming words in one line — often comes out with unnatural stress patterns. Simple end-line rhymes (ABAB or AABB) work best. Save your complex schemes for human vocalists who can deliver them. This is one of several [common Suno mistakes](./27-technique-10-suno-ai-mistakes.md) that waste credits.

**Archaic and uncommon vocabulary.** Words like "erstwhile," "betwixt," "hitherto," or "perchance" are low-frequency in Suno's training data. The model has less confident pronunciation mappings for them, leading to awkward delivery. Stick to words that appear regularly in modern pop, rock, hip-hop, and country music.

## Power Words Suno Handles Well

Just as some words cause problems, others consistently produce clear, emotionally resonant output. These are words Suno has seen thousands of times in its training data, with well-established phoneme mappings and natural melodic associations.

**Emotional core words:** love, heart, fire, rain, dream, night, light, soul, free, alive, broken, golden, forever, never, always. These words appear across every genre of popular music. Suno delivers them with confidence and natural inflection every time. They are your safest building blocks for hooks and chorus lines.

**Action words with clear imagery:** running, falling, burning, dancing, crying, flying, breaking, holding, fading, rising. These produce strong vocal delivery because they carry inherent emotional weight and have simple, predictable pronunciation. They also help Suno "understand" the emotional direction of a section.

**Sensory words:** cold, warm, bright, dark, loud, quiet, soft, rough, deep, high. These ground your lyrics in physical experience and tend to produce expressive vocal delivery. Suno maps these to tonal qualities — "dark" and "deep" often get lower, richer delivery while "bright" and "high" get lighter, more elevated vocal tones.

The pattern here is clear: **common, emotionally loaded, easy-to-pronounce words produce the best results.** This is not about dumbing down your lyrics. It is about using the vocabulary that works best with the tool. Combined with the right prompt formula and proper post-processing, great lyrics are the foundation for [making Suno music sound professional](https://undetectr.com/blog/make-suno-music-sound-professional).

## The Hook Formula: Simple, Repetitive, Melodic

The chorus hook is where your song lives or dies — in AI music and in traditional music alike. But in Suno, the hook has an additional constraint: it needs to be simple enough for the AI to deliver with maximum melodic impact on every repeat. Complex hooks with many words get different treatments each time the chorus appears, destroying consistency.

The formula that works: **6-10 words maximum for your core hook line.** This is the single phrase that defines the song. "We are the champions." "Don't stop believin'." "I will always love you." Notice the pattern — short, clear, emotionally direct. Suno locks onto these short phrases and generates consistent, strong melodic delivery every time.

Repetition within the chorus reinforces the hook. Write the hook line, then build 2-3 supporting lines around it, then return to the hook. A common structure: hook line, context line, context line, hook line (with slight variation). This gives Suno clear instructions: this phrase is the most important element, deliver it with maximum impact.

Avoid cramming your chorus with new information. The verse tells the story. The chorus delivers the emotional payload. If your chorus has 8 lines of unique content, it is really another verse. Cut it to 4-6 lines with repetition and watch the melodic quality jump. Suno generates its best melodies when it has a clear, repeatable phrase to anchor the section around.

## Writing Lyrics for Specific Genres

Each genre has distinct lyrical conventions that Suno recognizes and responds to. Writing pop lyrics for a hip-hop prompt (or vice versa) creates a mismatch that produces confused, unfocused output. Here is how to tailor your lyrics to each major genre.

### Pop

Pop lyrics should be immediately accessible. Use simple vocabulary, catchy phrasing, and universal themes (love, freedom, partying, heartbreak). Keep syllable counts at 6-8 per line. Rhyme schemes should be obvious — AABB or ABAB. The chorus should be the kind of phrase someone could sing after hearing it once. Avoid abstract concepts. Be direct and emotionally clear.

### Hip-Hop and Rap

Rap lyrics for Suno need to be rhythmic and punchy but not so densely packed that the AI cannot keep up. Aim for 10-14 syllables per line with consistent internal rhythm. Use end rhymes and simple internal rhymes. Avoid triple-time flows or polyrhythmic patterns — Suno tends to default to straightforward rhythmic delivery. Punchy, short words work better than multisyllabic vocabulary. Give breathing room with line breaks.

### Ballads

Ballad lyrics should flow like a conversation. Longer lines (8-12 syllables) with gentle, natural cadence. Emotional vocabulary is key — Suno responds well to words like "heart," "tears," "forever," and "remember" in ballad contexts. Avoid rapid-fire delivery. Let lines breathe with commas and natural pauses. The pacing should feel like someone telling a story, not performing a speech.

### EDM and Electronic

Electronic music lyrics should be minimal and repetitive. A single phrase repeated with slight variations can carry an entire EDM track. Think "one more time" or "feel the beat." Use 4-6 syllable phrases maximum. Repetition is not lazy in electronic music — it is essential. The vocals serve as another rhythmic instrument, not as storytelling. Avoid narrative lyrics. Focus on energy, feeling, and movement.

## Before and After: One Verse Rewritten for Suno V6

Here is every rule above applied to one verse. The “before” is the kind of verse that reads well on a page and that a general-purpose AI writer tends to produce. The “after” is the same idea rewritten for Suno to sing. This is an illustration of the method, not a measured test: the point is the edits, which you can see line by line.

#### Before: written for reading

[Verse]
Under the fluorescent hum of a 24/7 laundromat
I contemplate the ephemeral architecture of us
Your sweater still tumbling, a cotton memory in revolution
At 3:15 my phone says nothing, and nothing says enough

#### After: written for Suno V6

[Verse 1: soft, close vocal]
Twenty-four seven, the dryers hum
Your sweater's still spinning round and round
Three fifteen and you still won't come
And the quiet is the loudest sound

[Chorus: full band, backing vocals answer]
Come home (come home)
The light is on, the night is long
Come home (come home)
I'm folding up this song

What changed, and why:

1. **No digits.** “24/7” and “3:15” became “twenty-four seven” and “three fifteen”, so there is only one way to sing them.
2. **Line length cut in half.** The before runs about 15 to 18 syllables a line. The after runs 9, 9, 8, 9, close enough for Suno to hold one melodic pattern through the verse.
3. **Plain words.** “Ephemeral architecture” and “revolution” are low-frequency words in song lyrics. Dryers, a sweater and a silent phone carry the same feeling and are easy to pronounce.
4. **A rhyme scheme.** Hum and come, round and sound: ABAB end rhymes give the melody somewhere to land.
5. **A numbered, described section tag.** `[Verse 1: soft, close vocal]` tells V6 where the section starts and how to sing it, without a word of it being sung.
6. **A two-word hook with a sung echo.** “Come home” repeats, and the echo sits in parentheses so it comes back as a backing vocal rather than a second lead line.

The whole “after” sheet, verse and chorus, is 330 characters. A full song built the same way, with two verses, three choruses and a bridge, still comes in well under a third of the lyrics box.

## How to Fix Mispronounced and Swapped Words

When Suno sings a word wrong, the instinct is to regenerate the whole song. That costs 10 credits for two new takes and usually loses the take you liked. Work up this ladder instead, cheapest fix first.

1. **Check where your words went.** If Suno sang different lyrics entirely, you were probably in Simple Mode, which writes its own words. Paste your lyrics into the Lyrics box in Custom Mode.
2. **Respell only the word that fails.** Write it the way it sounds. Words with two pronunciations are the usual culprits: “live”, “read”, “tear”, “wind”. If “wind” keeps coming out as the weather, write “wynd”. Leave the rest of the line alone.
3. **Turn digits and acronyms into sung words.** “Three fifteen”, “twenty nineteen”, “D-M-V”. See [the lyrics box rules](#brackets-parentheses) above.
4. **Break up the hard parts.** A hyphen can split a long word into the syllables you want, and a vowel-heavy word between two consonant clusters gives the vocal room to breathe.
5. **Listen to both takes.** Every generation makes two songs. The word that fails in one is often fine in the other.
6. **On V6, ask for the one-word change.** Suno’s own example is “Change the lyric from ‘love’ to ‘light’”: the line changes and the rest of the song is meant to stay as it was. Keep the original, because early users report the result is not always faithful.
7. **On Pro or Premier, replace the section.** In the Song Editor, highlight the section, choose Edit Lyrics, change the words in the Replace Lyrics box and click Replace Section. Suno makes two versions of the section and builds a new full song around the one you pick. Our [Suno Editor guide](https://undetectr.com/blog/suno-editor) walks through it.
8. **Last resort: Reuse Prompt.** From the song’s menu, Reuse Prompt refills the form with your lyrics and style so you can fix the text and generate a new version. It is a new song, not a repair.

One V6 problem this ladder does not fully solve: V6 changing words you did not ask it to change. The Variety slider is the first thing to check. Suno says it adjusts and updates your style prompt, and that setting it to 0 keeps your style tags as typed. It is not a guarantee for the lyrics, though: a r/SunoAI post on 4 October 2026 was titled “Suno v6 changes my lyrics and Style with Variety Off.” Read the lyrics in the song details after every generation, before you build on a take.

## Suno V6: Fix One Line Without Regenerating

Until September 2026 the lyric workflow had one brutal rule: a bad line meant a new generation, and a new generation meant a new song. Suno V6, released on 9 September, changes that in three ways that matter for how you write.

![Suno V6 lyric editing flow: pick the line, say the change in plain language, the rest of the song stays the same](https://undetectr.com/blog/write-lyrics-for-suno-ai/edit-one-line.webp)

**1. You can change one lyric and keep everything else.** V6 accepts a plain-language instruction against an existing song: swap this phrase for that one, leave the rest intact. In practice that means you can write the whole lyric sheet with the techniques above, generate once, and then repair the single line that came out clipped, instead of re-rolling and losing the chorus you liked. Suno's own example is “Change the lyric from ‘love’ to ‘light’.” One caveat: early users report it is not reliable yet, with some edits coming back in the same voice but a different arrangement. Keep your original take. For section-level fixes on Pro and Premier, Replace Section in the Song Editor is the more controllable tool; the [fix ladder above](#fix-mispronunciations) puts the two in order.

**2. V6 stops doubling your lines.** The most consistent piece of good news on the launch threads was that V6 no longer repeats a verse or stacks ad-libs on top of a line unless you write them in. On older models the syllable-matching advice in this guide partly existed to stop that behaviour. On V6 you can write the ad-lib you want in parentheses, where it is sung as a backing vocal, and expect roughly that, nothing more. (An earlier version of this guide said brackets; brackets are for instructions and are not sung.)

**3. Long lyric sheets: use Max Mode, and keep length honest.** Suno describes Max Mode as an option that costs more credits and is best for songs longer than two minutes and for “keeping vocals and style consistent through the whole track.” Users have reported the problem it is meant to address: one r/SunoAI post on 17 September was titled “The first 2 minutes sound great, then the rest of the song sounds like a TIN CAN,” and another on 23 September described audio degrading toward the end of V6 songs “even with Max Mode.” If your lyric sheet runs past two verses, two choruses and a bridge, generate in Max Mode, and treat length as a quality decision, not just a character count.

Two things did not change. The conversational rule, syllable matching and structure tags matter as much on V6 as they did on v5.5, and several early testers report V6 *reads* dense lines rather than singing them, so a lyric written for the page still gets you flat delivery. And a V6 export still carries Suno's generator tag and clip id in the file, so the distribution step at the end of this guide is unchanged. ## Using ChatGPT and Claude for Suno Lyrics

Suno's own [Write with Suno](#can-suno-write-lyrics) is quicker for a first draft inside the app. ChatGPT and Claude are useful when you want to set detailed rules yourself, such as syllable counts per line and a fixed rhyme scheme — if you prompt them correctly. The default output from ChatGPT or Claude tends to be too literary, too complex, and too wordy for Suno. You need to constrain the output to match Suno's strengths.

Here is a prompt template that consistently produces good results: "Write lyrics for an AI music generator (Suno). The song should be [genre] about [topic]. Use simple, conversational vocabulary — write like you speak, not like poetry. Include structure tags in square brackets: [Verse 1], [Pre-Chorus], [Chorus], [Verse 2], [Bridge], [Outro]. Put backing vocals and ad-libs in parentheses and nothing else in parentheses. Write every number, time and year out as words. Keep 6-8 syllables per line for pop, 10-14 for rap. The chorus hook should be under 10 words. Avoid tongue twisters, unusual names, and archaic vocabulary. Use AABB or ABAB rhyme schemes."

The key instructions that make a difference: specifying that lyrics are for an AI music generator (this alone changes the output style), setting syllable count targets, requesting structure tags, and explicitly asking for simple vocabulary. Without these constraints, AI writing tools default to sophisticated literary output that sounds impressive on paper but produces garbled Suno vocals.

After getting the initial output, run a second pass: "Now count the syllables in each line. Make sure lines within each verse have consistent counts (within 1-2 syllables of each other). Replace any word over 4 syllables with a simpler alternative. Make the chorus hook 6-8 words maximum." This optimization pass catches the common issues that AI writers introduce — inconsistent line lengths and vocabulary that is too complex for vocal synthesis.

## Advanced: Phonetic Writing for Accent Effects

This technique is used by advanced Suno creators to achieve specific vocal effects that standard English spelling cannot produce. The principle: **spell words how they should sound, not how they are spelled.** Suno's vocal model converts text to phonemes before generating audio, so phonetic spelling lets you control the exact pronunciation.

For a Southern drawl effect, write "ah" instead of "I," "gonna" instead of "going to," "ain't" instead of "isn't," and "y'all" instead of "you all." For a British accent hint, drop "r" sounds at the end of words: "nevah" instead of "never," "forevah" instead of "forever." These substitutions push Suno's vocal delivery toward the accent without requiring any special settings.

Phonetic writing also helps with word emphasis. Writing "so-o-o" extends the vowel, producing a stretched delivery. "Na na na" or "la la la" produce melodic filler that sounds natural. "Ooh" and "aah" work as vocal adornments between lines. These are not gimmicks — they are the same techniques human songwriters use when writing for real vocalists. The difference is that with Suno, phonetic spelling is the *only* way to achieve these effects.

Use phonetic writing sparingly. A few strategic substitutions per song add character. Overusing them makes the entire lyric look like a dialect transcript and can confuse Suno's phoneme mapping. The sweet spot is 2-3 phonetic substitutions per verse that create a subtle accent feel without overwhelming the model.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can Suno write lyrics for you?

Yes, in three places. Simple Mode writes the lyrics for you from a one-line description of the song. In Custom Mode, Write with Suno generates lyrics from a topic, with a choice of the ReMi lyric model for edgier, less expected lines or a classic style, and you can co-write line by line before you generate. Since July 2026 the lyrics editor also lets you save examples of your own lyrics as a Lyricist, edit lines with plain-language requests such as "make this line funnier", and highlight a word for rhymes. Suno says writing lyrics is part of the free plan.

### How do I get Suno to write lyrics for me?

If the words do not matter, use Simple Mode: describe the song and Suno writes the lyrics, picks a style and makes two takes. If you want to read and change the lyrics first, switch to Custom Mode, use Write with Suno in the lyrics area, describe the topic, pick ReMi or the classic style, then edit the lines before you add a Style and press Create. Check the draft against the syllable and formatting rules in this guide before you spend credits on it.

### Why can't I find Custom Mode in Suno?

On the web, several walkthroughs of the Suno V6 Create page show the toggle at the top reading Simple, Advanced and Sounds. Advanced is the mode that used to be labelled Custom: it has the Lyrics box, the Style box and the extra options. The iOS app and Suno's help articles still call it Custom.

### What is the character limit for Suno lyrics?

Suno does not publish one. Users and developers who have read the Create form in 2026 report 5,000 characters for the Lyrics box and 1,000 for the Style box. A three-minute song uses roughly 900 to 1,500 characters, so the box is rarely the real limit. Song length is: Suno says all three V6 models make up to 8 minutes per generation, and it recommends Max Mode, which costs more credits, for songs longer than two minutes.

### What is the best lyric length for Suno AI?

For a standard 3-minute song, aim for 2 verses of 4-8 lines each, a chorus of 4-6 lines repeated 2-3 times, an optional pre-chorus of 2-4 lines, a bridge of 4 lines, and an outro of 2-4 lines. Total lyrics should be roughly 150-250 words. Going longer risks rushed or blurred sections, and on V6 users report sound quality dropping in the back half of long songs. Going shorter can produce extended instrumental sections or looped content.

### What is the difference between brackets and parentheses in Suno lyrics?

Square brackets hold instructions that Suno reads but does not sing: section labels such as [Verse 1] or [Chorus] and short performance notes. Parentheses are sung, usually as a backing vocal or echo under the lead line, which is where ad-libs belong. Never put an instruction in parentheses, because it can be sung as a word. Suno has not published an official tag list; these are the conventions its users rely on, and on V6 a tag works as a strong hint rather than a command.

### How do I stop Suno mispronouncing a word?

Respell only the word that fails, the way it sounds. Write numbers, times and years as words ("three fifteen", "twenty nineteen"), spell out acronyms you want sung letter by letter ("D-M-V"), and break up heavy consonant clusters. If one word still comes out wrong in a take you like, V6 can change a single lyric from a plain-language instruction, and on Pro and Premier you can highlight the section in the Song Editor, edit the lyrics and use Replace Section, which gives you two versions to choose from.

### Does punctuation matter in Suno lyrics?

Punctuation has a subtle but real effect on Suno output. Commas create brief pauses, periods create longer breaks, and ellipses (...) can produce trailing vocal effects. Exclamation marks can push Suno toward more energetic delivery. However, do not over-punctuate — Suno handles natural sentence flow better than heavily punctuated text. Use line breaks as your primary pacing tool rather than excessive punctuation.

### How do I get Suno to emphasize certain words?

Suno does not have a built-in emphasis syntax, but there are workarounds. Placing a word on its own line gives it more weight. Repeating a word or phrase signals importance to the model. Using ALL CAPS occasionally can push toward stronger vocal delivery, though results vary. The most reliable method is placing the word you want emphasized at the start of a line or at the end of a line before a structural break.

### Can I use copyrighted lyrics in Suno?

You should not use copyrighted lyrics in Suno for any track you plan to distribute or monetize. Suno's terms of service prohibit using content that infringes on third-party intellectual property, and with V6 Suno says it screens uploaded audio files and lyrics for unauthorized use. Even if the AI output sounds different from the original, using copyrighted lyrics as input creates legal exposure. If you want a similar vibe to a specific song, describe the mood, theme, and style to an AI writing tool and ask for original lyrics inspired by that feeling.

### Can I change one lyric in Suno V6 without regenerating the whole song?

Yes. Suno lists it as a V6 feature: give the song a plain-language instruction such as "Change the lyric from 'love' to 'light'" and the rest of the track stays as it was. Early users report it is inconsistent, with some edits coming back with a different arrangement, so keep your original take. Launch-week users also reported that V6 stops repeating verses and stacking ad-libs you did not write, and Suno recommends Max Mode for songs longer than two minutes.

### Is ChatGPT or Claude better than Suno's own lyric writer?

They do different jobs. Suno's Write with Suno is fastest when you only need a starting draft inside the app. ChatGPT or Claude is better when you want to set the rules yourself: syllable targets per line, a rhyme scheme, structure tags and plain vocabulary. Tell them the lyrics are for an AI music generator that reads text literally, then rewrite the result in your own words, especially for anything you plan to release.

### How many syllables per line should I write for Suno?

For pop and rock, aim for 6-10 syllables per line. For rap and hip-hop, 10-16 syllables per line works well. For ballads, 8-12 syllables per line creates a natural flow. The critical rule is consistency: if your first verse line has 8 syllables, keep the other lines in that verse close to 8. Suno generates melodies based on the syllable pattern of the first lines it encounters, so inconsistent counts force awkward melodic stretches or compressions.
