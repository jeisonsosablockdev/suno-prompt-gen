## Mistake #1: Writing Vague Prompts

![Table of the 10 most common Suno AI mistakes paired with the fix for each one](https://undetectr.com/blog/suno-ai-mistakes/common-mistakes-fixes.webp)

This is the single most common mistake new Suno users make. They type something like "upbeat pop song about love" and wonder why the output sounds like every other AI-generated track on the internet. The reason is simple: **vague prompts pull toward the statistical center** of Suno's training data.

When you give Suno a generic prompt, it has to fill in every creative decision itself — the tempo, the instrumentation, the vocal style, the production aesthetic, the song structure. It does this by averaging across millions of examples. The result is a track that sounds competent but completely forgettable. It is the musical equivalent of clip art.

The fix is specificity. Instead of "upbeat pop song about love," try "bright indie pop, female vocal, breathy tone, acoustic guitar and synth pads, 118 BPM, verse-chorus-verse-bridge-chorus structure, about finding confidence after a breakup." Every specific detail you add pushes the output away from the generic center and toward something with actual character. For 100+ proven prompts across every genre, see our [complete Suno prompts guide](./29-technique-general-prompts-guide.md). For a worked example in one genre, our [Suno instrumental prompts for ambient and sleep music](./13-genre-prompts-ambient.md) break a prompt into five slots, from substyle to use-case.

Think of it this way: Suno is not a mind reader. It is a pattern completion engine. The more specific your pattern, the more interesting the completion. Professional producers who use Suno regularly report that **prompt specificity is the single biggest factor** in output quality — more important than any other setting or technique.

## Mistake #2: Ignoring Tempo and Key Specifications

Tempo and key are not minor details — they are **emotional levers** that fundamentally change what your track communicates. A song at 72 BPM in a minor key creates an entirely different emotional universe than the same lyrics at 128 BPM in a major key. Yet most Suno users leave these decisions entirely to the AI.

When you do not specify tempo, Suno defaults to whatever BPM is most statistically common for the genre you described. For pop, that is roughly 100-120 BPM. For hip-hop, around 80-90 BPM. These defaults are fine if you want average results. They are terrible if you want a specific mood or energy level.

Key matters just as much. Minor keys create tension, melancholy, and emotional depth. Major keys convey brightness, happiness, and resolution. Specific keys even have tonal associations — D minor is often described as deeply emotional, while G major feels warm and open. Specifying the key in your style field gives Suno a concrete tonal foundation to build from.

The fix: always include BPM and key in your style field. If you are unsure what tempo or key to use, reference tracks you admire. Look up their BPM and key using any free online tool, then use those values as a starting point. The combination of "120 BPM, key of E minor" takes two seconds to type and dramatically narrows Suno's output toward your intended mood.

## Mistake #3: Not Using Style Tags Effectively

Suno's style field is the most powerful control you have over the sonic direction of your output. But most users treat it like a simple genre selector — they type "pop" or "rock" and call it done. This barely scratches the surface of what style tags can do.

**Style tags stack.** You can combine genre, sub-genre, mood, production style, and era into a single style field. For example: "dreamy shoegaze, lo-fi production, ethereal female vocals, reverb-drenched guitars, 1990s aesthetic" gives Suno five distinct sonic dimensions to work with. Each tag narrows the output space and pushes the result toward something specific and intentional.

The hierarchy matters too. Tags listed first carry more weight in Suno's interpretation. If you write "jazz, electronic" you get jazz with electronic elements. If you write "electronic, jazz" you get electronic music with jazz influences. The order is not arbitrary — it is a priority system.

Production-era tags are particularly powerful and underused. Adding "1970s analog warmth" or "2020s hyperpop production" or "1990s lo-fi bedroom recording" gives Suno a specific sonic palette to draw from. These era-based descriptors influence everything from EQ curves to stereo width to the amount of compression applied. Experiment with era tags to find the sonic character that matches your vision. ## Mistake #4: Using Simple Mode Instead of Custom Mode

![Side-by-side comparison of Suno Simple Mode versus Custom Mode showing Custom Mode gives more control at the same credit cost](https://undetectr.com/blog/suno-ai-mistakes/simple-vs-custom-mode.webp)

Simple Mode exists for people who want to type a sentence and get a song. It is fast, it is easy, and it produces mediocre results almost every time. The reason is fundamental: **Simple Mode forces you to give up control** over the three elements that matter most — lyrics, structure, and detailed style direction.

In Simple Mode, you provide a text description and Suno generates everything: the lyrics, the melody, the arrangement, the structure. You are essentially asking the AI to make every creative decision for you. The output reflects that — it is competent but generic, because Suno optimizes for broad appeal rather than specific artistic intent.

[Custom Mode](./26-technique-custom-mode-guide.md) changes the equation entirely. You write your own lyrics (or paste them in), define the style field with stacked tags, and control the song structure with section markers. This gives you influence over the three pillars of any song: what is being said, how it sounds, and how it is organized.

The common objection is "I am not a lyricist." You do not need to be. Even basic, conversational lyrics that you write yourself will produce better vocal performances than Suno's auto-generated lyrics. And you can use ChatGPT, Claude, or any other LLM to help draft lyrics — then paste them into Custom Mode. The combination of human-directed lyrics with Suno's musical generation is dramatically better than letting Suno handle everything.

Custom Mode costs the same number of credits as Simple Mode. There is literally no downside to using it.

## Mistake #5: Not Using Structural Tags

Structural tags are what separate chaotic AI output from actual songs. Tags like **[Verse]**, **[Chorus]**, **[Bridge]**, **[Outro]**, and **[Instrumental Break]** tell Suno exactly how to organize your track. Without them, Suno makes its own structural decisions — and those decisions are often repetitive, meandering, or abrupt.

A common problem without structural tags is the "endless verse" — Suno generates verse after verse without ever hitting a chorus or any kind of dynamic shift. Another is the abrupt ending, where the track just stops because Suno ran out of generation length without reaching a natural conclusion. Structural tags solve both problems by giving the AI a roadmap.

The basic template that works for most genres is: [Intro] [Verse 1] [Chorus] [Verse 2] [Chorus] [Bridge] [Chorus] [Outro]. This standard pop structure gives your track clear dynamics — tension in the verses, release in the choruses, a shift at the bridge, and a clean ending. You can modify this for any genre, but having *some* structure is always better than having none.

Advanced structural tags include [Pre-Chorus], [Post-Chorus], [Instrumental Break], [Drop], [Build], and [Ad-lib]. Suno V5 recognizes all of these and adjusts its generation accordingly. The [Instrumental Break] tag is particularly useful for creating breathing room in a track and adding variety. Place structural tags on their own lines in the lyrics field, and Suno will treat them as section markers rather than lyrics to be sung.

## Mistake #6: Writing Overly Complex Lyrics

This mistake trips up creative writers who bring their poetry skills to Suno without understanding the AI's limitations. **Conversational, natural-sounding language gets clearer vocals.** Complex rhyme schemes, multi-syllabic words, dense metaphors, and unusual phrasing cause Suno to stumble — producing mumbled words, garbled consonants, or outright gibberish.

The reason is technical. Suno's vocal synthesis works best with common phonetic patterns. When it encounters unusual word combinations or complex syllable structures, the model has fewer training examples to draw from. The result is less confident vocal rendering — the AI equivalent of a singer struggling with unfamiliar lyrics.

This does not mean your lyrics need to be simplistic or dumbed down. It means they need to be *singable*. Read your lyrics out loud before pasting them into Suno. If any line feels awkward to say naturally, it will sound worse when Suno tries to sing it. For the complete breakdown of what works, see our guide on [writing lyrics for Suno AI](./25-technique-write-lyrics-for-suno-ai.md).

One specific technique: avoid cramming too many syllables into a single line. Suno maps lyrics to melodic phrases, and an overcrowded line forces the model to rush through words or compress them together. Give each line breathing room. If a line has more than 12-15 syllables, consider splitting it across two lines. The resulting vocal performance will be dramatically cleaner.

## Mistake #7: Expecting Perfect Results on the First Try

AI music generation is inherently probabilistic. Every generation produces a different result even with identical inputs. This is a feature, not a bug — but many users treat it as a frustration. They generate one track, decide it is not good enough, and conclude that Suno does not work.

Professional Suno users approach generation as an **iterative process**. They generate 5-10 versions of the same concept, listen through all of them, and cherry-pick the best elements. Maybe version 3 has a great chorus but weak verses. Maybe version 7 has perfect vocal tone but a boring melody. The goal is not to get one perfect track on the first try — it is to find the best output across multiple attempts.

[Suno V5's](https://undetectr.com/blog/suno-v5-review) Studio features make this iteration even more powerful. You can extend a track from a specific point, regenerate individual sections, and combine elements from different generations. The Alternates feature lets you generate multiple versions of the same section and pick the best one without starting over. This is how the best Suno creators work — they treat generation as a selection process, not a single-shot lottery.

Set a credit budget for each project and expect to spend it. If you are on the Pro plan with 2,500 monthly credits, plan to spend 100-200 credits on a single finished track. That is 10-20 generations, which gives you plenty of material to select from. The final track will be dramatically better than any single first-attempt generation.

## Mistake #8: Not Tracking What Works

Most Suno users generate hundreds of tracks over weeks and months without ever recording which prompts, styles, and settings produced their best results. This means they are effectively starting from scratch every session, relearning the same lessons and repeating the same mistakes.

The fix is dead simple: **keep a prompt tracker.** This can be a spreadsheet, a Notion database, a text file — anything where you log your inputs and rate your outputs. For each generation, record: the style field text, a summary of the lyrics, which structural tags you used, the genre and mood you were targeting, and a quality rating from 1 to 5.

Within a few weeks of consistent tracking, patterns emerge. You will discover that certain style tag combinations consistently produce good results for you. You will notice that specific vocal descriptors work better than others. You will build a personal library of proven prompts that you can remix and recombine for new projects.

The most productive Suno creators treat their prompt tracker as their most valuable asset. A proven prompt formula that reliably produces 4-out-of-5 quality output is worth more than any technique or trick. It converts the randomness of AI generation into a repeatable, predictable workflow. Start tracking today — your future self will thank you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the single most impactful Suno AI mistake to fix first?

Using Simple Mode instead of Custom Mode is the biggest lever. Custom Mode gives you control over lyrics, structure tags, and style fields that collectively determine 80% of your output quality. Switching to Custom Mode alone will dramatically improve your results even if you change nothing else about your workflow.

### Does Custom Mode use more credits than Simple Mode?

No. Custom Mode and Simple Mode cost the same number of credits per generation. There is no credit penalty for using Custom Mode. Since Custom Mode produces better results, you will actually save credits by needing fewer re-generations to get a track you are happy with.

### Do structural tags like [Verse] and [Chorus] work in Simple Mode?

No. Structural tags only work in Custom Mode where you write your own lyrics. In Simple Mode, Suno generates both the lyrics and structure automatically, and any tags you try to include in a text prompt will be ignored or treated as regular text. You must use Custom Mode to take advantage of structural tags.

### What is the best way to track which Suno prompts work?

Create a simple spreadsheet or document with columns for the style field text, lyrics snippet, structural tags used, genre, mood, and a quality rating from 1 to 5. After every generation session, log what you used and rate the output. Within a few weeks you will have a personal library of proven prompt combinations that consistently produce good results for your specific genres.

### Does re-generating a Suno track cost additional credits?
